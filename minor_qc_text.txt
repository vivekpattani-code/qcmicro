Introduction:
Food labels are an essential part of consumer rights and food safety regulations. In India, food labelling is governed by the Food Safety and Standards (Packaging and Labelling) Regulations, notified under the FSSAI (Food Safety and Standards Authority of India). These labels provide critical information to consumers about the nature, quality, safety, and nutritional value of the food product. The label also ensures that the product meets legal standards and allows traceability and accountability in the food supply chain.

PRACTICAL 2
Microscopic examination of food products.
Aim: To examine food samples under a microscope for the presence of microorganisms, foreign matter, or structural components to assess microbiological and physical quality.
Introduction:
Food can harbour a wide range of microorganisms such as bacteria, yeasts, molds, and spores, as well as foreign matter like insect fragments, starch granules, or plant tissues. These contaminants may enter during production, processing, packaging, or storage. Microscopic examination serves as a rapid, preliminary technique for the detection of such contaminants before performing more elaborate microbiological or biochemical analyses.
This technique is especially useful for:
* Detecting microbial spoilage
* Identifying food adulteration
* Visualizing structural components of food
* Screening powdered or dried food products for insect fragments, mold, etc.
It is used in food quality control labs, as per standards laid down by bodies like FSSAI, BIS, AOAC, and USP.
Principle:
A small quantity of the food sample is diluted or emulsified, and a thin smear is prepared on a microscope slide. Depending on the expected contaminants, appropriate stains are applied (e.g., methylene blue for bacteria, lactophenol cotton blue for fungi, iodine for starch granules). The stained or unstained smear is observed under a microscope to detect and identify microorganisms or physical contaminants.
Requirements:
* Selected food samples (milk, bread, spices, flour, etc.)
* Clean glass slides and cover slips
* Light microscope (with 10x, 40x, and 100x objectives)
* Inoculating loop or sterile swabs
* Distilled water or normal saline
* Dropper or micropipette
* Staining reagents:
o Methylene Blue   for bacterial cells
o Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB)   for fungi
o Iodine Solution   for starch granules
* Forceps, scalpel, mortar & pestle
* Bunsen burner or spirit lamp

Procedure:
A. Sample Preparation:
* Solid samples (e.g., bread, flour):
* Grind a small amount in sterile saline or distilled water using mortar and pestle.
* Prepare a suspension.
* Liquid samples (e.g., milk, juice):
* Use directly or dilute if necessary.
* Powdered samples (e.g., spices):
* Suspend in sterile water and allow large particles to settle.
B. Slide Preparation & Staining:
1. Place a drop of suspension on a clean slide.
2. Make a thin smear and allow it to air dry.
3. Fix the smear by gently passing the slide over a flame.
4. Stain as follows:
* Methylene Blue: 1 2 minutes ? rinse ? air dry ? observe (for bacteria)
* Lactophenol Cotton Blue: Apply drop ? cover slip ? observe directly (for molds)
* Iodine Solution: Apply ? observe immediately (for starch or plant tissue)

C. Microscopic Observation:
* Start with low power (10x) to locate the area of interest.
* Use high power (40x) or oil immersion (100x) for detailed observation.

Note:
* Cell morphology: cocci, bacilli, yeast cells, fungal hyphae
* Staining reaction
* Presence of debris, starch granules, or foreign matter

PRACTICAL 3
Aerobic plate counts of the given food products.
Aim: To determine the total viable count (TVC) of aerobic mesophilic bacteria present in a given food sample using the pour plate method, following FSSAI microbiological standards.
Introduction:
The Total Aerobic Mesophilic Plate Count, commonly referred to as the Aerobic Plate Count (APC) or Standard Plate Count, is the most widely used general indicator of bacterial populations in food. This method does not differentiate types of bacteria. It is only used to obtain general information on the sanitary quality of products, manufacturing practices, raw materials, processing conditions, handling practices and shelf life.
This method is applicable to those food product categories and their subcategories as mentioned in the Appendix B tables of Food Safety and Standards (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, 2011 and amendments (Gazette notifications) issued from time to time.
Method No. FSSAI 15.001:2024
Principle: A specified quantity of homogenised food sample is either dispensed into an empty Petri dish, mixed with a specified molten agar medium or surface plated on a solid agar culture medium.  Other plates are prepared under the same conditions using decimal dilutions of the test sample or the initial suspension. The plates are incubated under aerobic conditions at 30   1  C for 72 h. The number of microorganisms per gram or millilitre of sample is calculated from the number of colonies obtained on the plates containing fewer than 300 colonies for 90-100mm plates and 730 colonies for 140mm plates.
Requirement:
* Food sample (e.g., milk, curd, juice, etc.)
* Sterile Peptone water (0.1%)
* Sterile Plate Count Agar (PCA) plates
* Sterile pipettes (1 ml and 10 ml)
* Sterile test tubes
* Bunsen burner or spirit lamp
* Incubator
Procedure:
1. Sample Preparation:
    a. Homogenise 10 g or 10 ml of food sample in 90 ml of sterile 0.1% peptone water to make a 10?  dilution.
    b. Prepare serial decimal dilutions up to 10?? depending on expected microbial load.
2. Plate Preparation (Pour Plate Method):
a. Take 1 ml from the appropriate dilution and transfer it into a sterile Petri dish.
b. Pour ~15 20 ml of molten Plate Count Agar (PCA) (cooled to 45 50 C) into the dish.
c. Gently swirl to mix thoroughly.
d. Allow the agar to solidify.
3. Incubation:
a. Invert the plates and incubate at 30 C for 48   2 hours.

Observation:
* After incubation, count colonies on plates with 30 to 300 CFU.
* Calculate the number of colony-forming units per gram or mL of sample.

PRACTICAL 4
Testing for pathogens in food products.
Aim: To detect the presence of coliform bacteria in water using selective media as per FSSAI guidelines.
Introduction: Coliforms are a broad group of aerobic or facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped, gram-negative non-spore forming bacteria which can ferment lactose with the production of acid and gas when incubated at 32 37 C. Coliform are members of Enterobacteriacae family. Most common genera are E. coli, Citrobacter, Enterobacter, Klebsiella and Hafnia. This method applies to those food product categories and their subcategories as mentioned in the Appendix B tables of Food Safety and Standards (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, 2011 and amendments (Gazette notifications) issued from time to time.
Method No. FSSAI 15.004:2024
Principle: Violet red bile lactose agar (VRBL) medium is used for enumeration of coliforms. VRBL contains selective inhibitors - inhibit the accompanying gram-positive and unrelated flora. Coliforms rapidly ferment lactose and produce red colonies surrounded by red purple halo.  Lactose non-fermenters and late lactose fermenters produce pale colonies. Confirmation is carried out in brilliant green bile broth medium, which is also having selective components for the growth of coliforms.

PRACTICAL 5
Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing.
Aim: To evaluate the effectiveness of antimicrobial preservatives in a pharmaceutical product by assessing their ability to reduce microbial load over time, as per USP <51> guidelines.
Introduction:
Microbial contamination of pharmaceutical and cosmetic products poses a serious threat to product safety and patient health. Many of these products, especially those that are aqueous or used over extended periods (e.g., eye drops, creams, syrups), are prone to microbial growth once opened or during storage. To mitigate this, antimicrobial preservatives are added to inhibit or kill a wide range of microorganisms that may be introduced inadvertently during manufacturing or by the user.
The United States Pharmacopoeia (USP) outlines a standard test method in Chapter <51> Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing (AET) to evaluate the capability of a product's preservative system to resist microbial contamination. This test is mandatory for many pharmaceuticals, personal care, and cosmetic formulations to ensure they maintain their microbiological integrity throughout their shelf life.
In this test, the product is intentionally inoculated with a high level of specific microorganisms representing bacteria (Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), yeast (Candida albicans), and mold (Aspergillus brasiliensis). The survival of these organisms is monitored over 0, 7, 14, and 28 days. A log reduction in microbial count within specific timeframes is required for the preservative system to be deemed effective. AET not only assesses the broad-spectrum activity of the preservatives but also ensures that the product is safe for use under expected storage and handling conditions. It is an essential part of product stability studies and regulatory submissions, and its results directly influence product approval and consumer safety.
Why It's Important:
* Ensures microbiological stability of the product during storage and use.
* Verifies efficacy of preservatives against a range of common contaminants.
* Aids in regulatory compliance (e.g., US FDA, USP, FSSAI, CDSCO).
* Helps manufacturers choose or optimise preservative systems.
This test involves deliberate inoculation of the product with specified microorganisms and monitoring their survival over 28 days. The test assures that the preservative system is capable of controlling microbial contamination, thereby ensuring product safety, stability, and regulatory compliance.
Principle: Antimicrobial Effectiveness Testing determines whether a product s preservative system can prevent microbial growth. Test microorganisms are introduced into the product, and their survival is monitored over a period (up to 28 days). A significant reduction in microbial count indicates preservative efficacy.

PRACTICAL 6
Sterility testing of a pharmaceutical Product.
Aim: To perform sterility testing of a pharmaceutical product using the Direct Inoculation Method to ensure the product is free from viable contaminating microorganisms.
Introduction:
Sterility testing is a critical microbiological quality control procedure used to ensure that pharmaceutical and medical products claimed to be sterile are indeed free from viable contaminating microorganisms. This includes products such as injectable preparations, ophthalmic solutions, surgical dressings, sutures, implants, and certain medical devices. 
The purpose of this test is not to determine the bioburden but to detect the presence or absence of microorganisms. As these products are intended for parenteral or other sterile administration, even a single viable organism can pose serious health risks including sepsis, endophthalmitis, or other systemic infections. Sterility testing is performed under strict aseptic conditions to prevent false positives resulting from environmental contamination during testing. The test must be conducted in a Class 100 (ISO 5) cleanroom or inside a laminar airflow hood in a controlled microbiological laboratory environment.
According to major pharmacopoeias (USP <71>, IP, BP, EP), two primary methods are prescribed for sterility testing:
* Membrane Filtration Method   suitable for filterable liquids and products containing bacteriostatic/fungistatic substances.
* Direct Inoculation Method   suitable for small-volume or non-filterable products by direct inoculation into sterile media.

Principle:
Two types of culture media are employed:
Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTM): Supports the growth of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. Soybean Casein Digest Medium (SCDM): Supports the growth of fungi (yeasts and molds) and aerobic bacteria.
These media are incubated at two different temperatures:
* FTM at 30 35 C
* SCDM at 20 25 C 
for a period of 14 days, allowing ample time for the growth of even slow-growing organisms.

Sterility test results are accepted only if no turbidity or visible microbial growth is observed in either medium at the end of the incubation period. The procedure must be validated and accompanied by positive and negative controls to confirm reliability. Overall, sterility testing is a regulatory requirement and a batch release criterion for sterile pharmaceutical products as per FSSAI, CDSCO, USP, IP, and WHO-GMP regulations. 
Requirement
* Test pharmaceutical product (injectable, eye drop, etc.)
* Sterile Soybean Casein Digest Medium (SCDM / TSB)
* Sterile Fluid Thioglycollate Medium (FTM)
* Laminar airflow cabinet
* Sterile syringes and needles
* Incubator (20 25 C and 30 35 C)
* Personal protective equipment (gloves, mask, gown)
* Alcohol (70% IPA)

Procedure: Direct Inoculation Method
1. Aseptic Area Preparation:
* Perform the test under a laminar airflow hood.
* Disinfect all working surfaces and allow airflow for 30 minutes before use.

2. Sample Preparation:
* Wipe the ampoules/vials with 70% alcohol.
* Open using aseptic techniques.

3. Inoculation:
* Transfer the specified volume (as per pharmacopeia) of the product directly into:
* FTM for detection of anaerobic and aerobic bacteria.
* SCDM for detection of fungi and aerobic bacteria.
* Inoculate into two separate test tubes or flasks (one for each medium).

4. Incubation:
Incubate:
* FTM at 30 35 C
* SCDM at 20 25 C
* For 14 days under observation.
5. Observation:
* Observe the media daily for any signs of turbidity (indicating microbial growth).
* Perform gram staining and subculture if growth is observed.

PRACTICAL 7
Evaluation of Surface sterilization by Swab testing.
Aim: To assess the microbial load on different laboratory and industrial surfaces before and after sterilization using the swab testing technique.
Introduction
In any environment where microbial contamination must be controlled such as pharmaceutical cleanrooms, hospitals, food processing units, microbiology labs, and sterile manufacturing units it is essential to assess the effectiveness of surface sterilization procedures. Even after cleaning, surfaces may retain viable microorganisms that can compromise product quality and safety or pose a risk of infection.
One of the standard techniques for evaluating the microbial cleanliness of surfaces is swab testing, also known as the contact swab method. This technique involves using a sterile swab to collect microbial samples from a defined area of a surface before and/or after disinfection. The swab is then cultured in a suitable growth medium (broth or agar) under appropriate incubation conditions. The resulting microbial growth, usually expressed in colony-forming units (CFU), reflects the microbial load on that surface.
Swab testing can detect a variety of microorganisms such as: Bacteria, Yeasts and molds (especially in moist environments)
By comparing microbial loads before and after surface sterilization, this method helps validate the efficacy of disinfection protocols, identify potential contamination sources, and ensure compliance with regulatory standards such as those from the FSSAI, WHO, USP, and GMP guidelines.
Principle:
The swab method collects potential microbial contaminants from sterilized surfaces. When these swabs are cultured, any surviving microorganisms will grow on the nutrient medium. A clean surface will show no microbial growth, indicating effective sterilization. Presence of colonies indicates incomplete disinfection or contamination.



Requirements:
* Sterile swabs.
* Sterile Normal saline or sterile peptone water tubes.
* Sterile Petriplate.
* Sterile Plate count agar & Potato Dextrose agar. 
* Sterile Test tubes & pipettes.
* Disinfectant: _________________________
Procedure:
1. Select a defined surface area (e.g., 10 cm   10 cm) before and after (surface becomes dry) sterilization.
2. Moisten a sterile swab in sterile saline.
3. Swab the surface area in a zig-zag manner horizontally and vertically, rotating the swab.
4. Transfer the swab into a sterile tube with sterile normal saline or sterile peptone water.
5. Pour Plate technique: Transfer 1ml of inoculum from normal saline or peptone water to sterile petriplates and add molten plate count agar & potato dextrose agar. 
6. Allow the agar to solidify.
7. Incubate the plates at plate count agar 37 C for 24 48 hours & potato dextrose agar at 22.5 C for 72 hours.
8. Observe and record colony growth (CFU).
9. Compare results before and after sterilization.
